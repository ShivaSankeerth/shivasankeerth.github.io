The first advice anyone gives a team building with language models is "you need evals." It is good advice. It is also where most teams stop thinking, and that is the problem. A bad eval is worse than no eval, because it gives you a number to point at while the actual behavior drifts underneath you. Over the last year I have watched a lot of dashboards stay green while the product got worse. So before I write about how to build evaluation that holds up, I want to be honest about how much of it does not.

## The scorecard broke

For a long time the public benchmarks did real work. They sorted models, they tracked progress, and a jump on MMLU or HumanEval meant something. That stopped being reliable, and 2025 is the year it became impossible to ignore.

Two failure modes did most of the damage. The first is saturation. When the top frontier models sit within a point or two of each other near the ceiling of a benchmark, that benchmark has stopped measuring anything useful at the frontier. The signal has moved into the long tail of hard cases, and a single-number leaderboard rank no longer tells you which model is better for your task.

The second is contamination. A benchmark only measures reasoning if the model has not already seen the answers. Once questions, or text closely derived from them, leak into a training corpus, the model recalls rather than reasons, and the score measures memorization. This is not a rare edge case. It is the default fate of any popular static benchmark with a fixed question set, because the internet eventually swallows it and the next training run eats it. The structural fix is to keep the question set fresher than any training cutoff, which is why the credible efforts have moved toward dynamic, continuously refreshed evaluations rather than frozen snapshots.

The practical takeaway is blunt: a public leaderboard rank is a weak prior, not evidence about your system. If your evaluation strategy is "we use a well-known benchmark," you do not have an evaluation strategy.

## LLM-as-judge is a tool, not an oracle

The dominant pattern for evaluating open-ended output is to ask another model to grade it. I use LLM-as-judge constantly. It is cheap, it scales, and for filtering and first-pass screening it is genuinely good. The mistake is treating its scores as ground truth.

Most teams wire up a judge with generic criteria like "helpfulness" or "coherence" and never look back. Those metrics are easy to produce and hard to trust. The judge has biases that quietly corrupt your numbers: it tends to prefer longer answers, it can favor whichever response is presented first, and it will happily reward fluent text that is confidently wrong, because fluency is exactly what it is good at recognizing. If you have ever seen a judge score a polished hallucination above a terse correct answer, you have met this problem.

A judge you can trust takes work. Write specific, task-grounded rubrics instead of vanilla adjectives. Calibrate the judge against a set of human-labeled examples and measure agreement before you believe any of its aggregate numbers. Control for the obvious biases by randomizing position and normalizing for length. And keep a human in the loop for the high-stakes slices, because a judge is fine for triage and a poor substitute for expert verification where the cost of being wrong is real.

## Agents make all of this harder

Everything above is about evaluating a single response. The moment you are evaluating an agent that plans, calls tools, and runs for many steps, the ground gets much less stable.

The first trap is that an agent benchmark score entangles things you want to keep separate. The number you get out reflects the base model, but it also reflects the scaffold, the output parser, the tool definitions, and the environment the benchmark happens to ship with. Swap the parser or tighten the prompt and the same model posts a different score. So when a result moves, you often cannot tell whether the model got better or the harness around it did. If you are trying to make a decision about the model, that entanglement is poison, and you have to hold the surrounding system fixed to learn anything.

The second trap is even more insidious, and it shows up in anything that touches a live environment. An agent's reasoning can be locally sensible at every single step and still fail, because the final outcome depends on what the environment returned, not only on what the model decided. Run the same web task twice and the page changed, the API rate-limited you, or a search returned different results. Now your score is partly measuring the weather. Pass at 1 on a flaky environment is closer to a coin flip than a capability measurement, and averaging over a handful of runs does not save you if the variance dwarfs the effect you are looking for.

The third trap is that outcome-only grading hides the failures you most need to see. Pass or fail on the final answer tells you nothing about how the agent got there. An agent can stumble into the right answer through a broken process, or do everything right and get burned by one bad tool result at the end. Both look identical on a pass/fail metric, and both will mislead you about what to fix.

## What actually works

None of this means evaluation is hopeless. It means you have to build it deliberately instead of importing someone else's leaderboard. A few things have earned my trust.

Build your evals from your own production data and your real failure modes. The dataset that matters is the one drawn from the requests your users actually send and the specific ways your system has actually broken. That set cannot be contaminated, it cannot be gamed, and it measures the thing you are paid to get right. Mine your logs, label the failures, and turn each recurring failure into a permanent test case. Your eval suite should grow every time something breaks.

Separate trajectory metrics from outcome metrics, especially for agents. Measure whether the agent reached the goal, and separately measure how it got there. Progress-style metrics that compare the agent's actual path against an expected one, and step-level checks on whether the next tool call was the right one, expose reasoning failures that a final-answer score papers over. When the outcome is right but the trajectory is ugly, you have found a latent bug that will bite you in production even though the test passed.

Treat evaluation as regression testing, not as a vanity scoreboard. The point is not a single number that goes up and to the right. The point is a suite that fails loudly and specifically the moment a behavior you care about regresses, so you can catch it before your users do. Tie it to your release process and make a regression block a ship the same way a failing unit test does.

And control the variance before you read the numbers. Pin environment versions, seed what you can, run enough trials to see the spread rather than a single point, and report distributions instead of a lone average. If you cannot reproduce a score, you do not have a measurement, you have an anecdote.

## Evals tell you that something is broken

Here is the limit I keep running into, and it is the reason I am writing this. Good evaluation is necessary. It catches regressions, it kills bad ideas early, and it keeps you honest. But evaluation is fundamentally a measurement instrument. It tells you that your agent is failing and, if you built it well, roughly where. It does not make the agent succeed.

The teams I see struggling are usually trying to evaluate their way to reliability, adding more benchmarks and more judges as if a better thermometer would cure the fever. It will not. Once your evals are honest, the next problem is not measurement at all. It is the system the agent runs inside: the loop, the tools, the memory, the environment it acts in. That is the part that turns a capable model into a dependable one, and it is where I want to spend the next post.

Build the evals. Trust them only after you have earned the right to. And then go fix the thing they are pointing at.
